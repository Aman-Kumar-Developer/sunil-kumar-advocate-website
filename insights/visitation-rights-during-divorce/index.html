Visitation Rights During Divorce: Balancing Parental Access and Child Welfare

A contact schedule is not simply a measure of either parent's entitlement. During a divorce, arrangements for a child should be considered in light of the child's welfare and the circumstances of the family.

When parents are separating, time with a child can become a source of uncertainty and conflict. A useful starting point is to treat visitation as part of a child-centred arrangement—not as an automatic, identical entitlement for either parent or as a prize in the adults' dispute.

The legal route depends on the proceeding

For proceedings under the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955, section 26 allows the court to make orders and provisions concerning the custody, maintenance and education of minor children in a proceeding under that Act. The section also permits the court to make or vary orders in the circumstances it covers. Other proceedings may rely on different statutory provisions. The section should therefore be read within its own scope rather than as a complete statement of every custody route.

In Gaurav Nagpal v. Sumedha Nagpal, the Supreme Court explained that a child's welfare, rather than either parent's superior claim, guides custody decisions. That principle informs how a court approaches arrangements, but it does not prescribe a standard visiting timetable.

Access and welfare are considered together

Maintaining a meaningful relationship with both parents may be relevant to a child's well-being. How contact is arranged, however, depends on the child's needs and the facts the court can assess. Neither a parent seeking contact nor a parent with day-to-day care can assume an absolute outcome in every case.

Arrangements may need to address practical matters such as the child's age and routine, school commitments, distance between homes, handovers and communication. If safety or other significant concerns are raised, those concerns also require careful assessment; they cannot be resolved by a generic schedule.

What makes an arrangement workable?

A proposed plan is easier to evaluate when it is specific and centred on the child: when contact would occur, how transitions would work, how changes would be communicated and how the arrangement fits the child's routine. Clarity can reduce avoidable uncertainty, but agreement or a particular schedule is not guaranteed. Any proposal remains subject to the governing law and the court's assessment.

Parenting arrangements may need reconsideration as a child's circumstances change. A court order should be followed unless it is changed through an appropriate legal process.

The child's circumstances come first

There is no single schedule that is right for every family. The legal framework, the child's day-to-day needs and any relevant welfare or safety concerns all matter. A parent's access is considered in that context, not in isolation from the child.
